Lo-Fly Radio

Four tapes composed by a fruit fly. Every kick, snare, bass note, chord, and melody was chosen by a simulated network built from his real ear-to-song wiring. He learned from a tutor, then practiced on his own for bananas.

The night shift

Fourteen minutes of his radio: four songs in a row, each with an intro, three grooves, a breakdown, and the groove coming back, then a new song in a new mood.

The tapes

Each tape is six phrases of eight bars, a short song with an intro, grooves, and a breakdown that he chose himself, rendered by the app's own instruments. The grid under each tape is exactly what he played in one phrase, one character per sixteenth note. Kick o soft, x hard. Snare g ghost, X hit. Hat - soft, + accent. Bass R root, 5 fifth, O octave, 3 third, 7 seventh, a approach, s stop. Keys # full chord, ^ top notes. Lead digits are scale steps above the tonic (A = 10, B = 11), and . is a rest.

How he learned

His brain

1,771 neurons from the MaleCNS connectome of a male fruit fly, with 78,710 connections signed by predicted neurotransmitters. Sound and his own playing enter only through his ear and wing sensors. His choices are read only from premotor, song, and wing motor neurons, so the music has to cross real synapses.

In the app he names, beat by beat, the neurons that pushed hardest for each sound. Over 180 phrases, his basslines leaned most on vMS11, a courtship-song neuron in his nerve cord. Wing motor neurons, only 3% of the neurons he plays from, drove 11% of his melody choices, including hg2, which steers a wing.

Measured, not yet listened to

“Original” is the share of his drum, bass, and melody bars that never appear anywhere in 1,800 phrases of his tutor's playing.

Bananas come from a critic we wrote, so a high score means the music matches our idea of lofi. It does not prove the music sounds good; that takes listening, which has not happened yet. Across seven paired training runs, a fly with shuffled wiring imitated the tutor better in school every time, but after practice the two were equal. His real wiring changes how he learns, and it does not by itself make better music by our measure.

Hear him live

In the app he composes in real time, shows each neuron lighting up on the beat, writes a new song every eight phrases, keeps practicing while you listen, and takes bananas from you. The work is on the fly-brain branch.
